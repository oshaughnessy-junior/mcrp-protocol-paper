# Who is allowed to say it passed?

A green check records an event. It does not establish independence, competence, authorization, or scientific adequacy.

Scientific workflows often compress unlike judgments into one success state. An author's script completed. A hosted service reproduced the outputs. A statistician judged the model appropriate. A domain reviewer accepted the interpretation. These statements answer different questions and should not inherit one another's authority.

MCRP separates author evidence, machine observation, reviewer-controlled execution, and qualified scientific disposition. An attestation binds an exact claim version, acceptance-contract version, evidence-set digest, release digest, issuer role, and decision. Its scope and exclusions remain visible. A service may attest that outputs match a declared predicate; under the current protocol, it cannot silently turn that observation into a human scientific approval.

Imagine an external execution service reconstructs all declared figures. A methods reviewer accepts the numerical procedure for two claims but marks a third as underpowered. A domain reviewer accepts one interpretation, narrows another, and leaves a clinical implication outside scope. The result is a vector of typed decisions, not one badge. A reader can see which questions were answered, by whom, and which remain open.

Portability follows only when the target, schema, issuer identity, evidence references, signature, and status are independently resolvable. Even then, a signature establishes attribution and integrity—not competence or independence. Those require separately inspectable evidence and a named policy. Different servers, accounts, or agents under common control do not automatically create independent review.

This separation makes approval precise, but precision is not permanence. Evidence changes. Methods are corrected. Credentials are revoked. The next post asks how an approval can remain historically true without remaining currently reliable.
